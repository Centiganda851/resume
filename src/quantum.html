Going Quantum

Quantum Technologies are an emerging field that uses the physics learned in the twentieth century for practical computational problems. To make headway in this space, a good team requires knowledge of atomic physics, quantum mechanics, linear algebra, vector calculus, quantum chemistry, information technology, logic design, programming and the application of algorithms. Therefore, it should not come as a surprise that it takes a team of geniuses to design and find use cases for these systems that are so powerful, we are still finding use cases for them, and I want to be a part of this story.

As I went through school, I had four standout subjects: Mathematics, Physics, IT and Chemistry. How could I not appreciate the universality of Physics, the recipes of Chemistry, the ubiquity of technology or the language of Math that bound them all together? The problem arose in High School where only three could be taken at high level with the fourth relegated to standard. I had to make a choice and ultimately, Chemistry drew the short straw so that I could focus on a career in electrical engineering. Then one serendipitious day, while researching modern technologies for my IT class in 2021, I encountered two words: Quantum computing.

This was the first time I had encountered the word "Quantum". It means a discrete packet. However, the impact it had in my life was very analog. Here was the perfect fusion of my four favorite subjects in a field so nascent that I had the chance to get on the train just as it left the station. My response was immediate. I found a book: Quantum computing Progress and Prospects from the National Academies Press and went to work understanding the technology. In hindsight, the book was way over my head at the time but the more I learned, the more I wanted to learn. I enrolled in Qubit by Qubit summer camp and wrote a poor research essay attempting to justify why Quantum technology will define the next generation of computers.

Once the essay was finished in late 2021, I entered my quantum winter. Some gifted people are able to juggle their passions and schoolwork, or have their passions supported by their school work, but my priorities shifted when I enrolled in Union University. I was an electrical engineer in training and that should have been it. However, in the Fall of 2023, I picked up a minor in Physics. I had liked the classes well enough and having lost Chemistry, I was not willing to lose it too. This seemingly small decision had a massive impact on the rest of my college journey because it led me to Quantum Mechanics, Spring 2023. With the beautiful elegance of the Hydrogen atom revealed, the quantum winter was over.

Now that I had an undergraduate-level understanding of the physical qubit layer, the race was on to find a way to break into the industry. Studying electromagnetics that Fall gave me that in by going into detail about photon travel. This is crucial to know to understand quantum gates and flying qubits. However, even with the study of the underlying math, I was still sorely lacking in one crucial aspect of quantum technologies: The actual programming. As mentioned earlier, engineers work in teams so that each member can specialize in what they are best at. However, as someone still outside the industry, it was important for me to generalize my skills and find an in.

Therefore, in the spring of 2025, I signed up for iQuHack 2025, my first ever hackathon, and my first time attempting to build software to solve a problem. My team chose the challenge of factoring semiprime numbers, which are the product of two prime numbers. It was a resounding personal success, not because we won the challenge, but because we had a functioning algorithm at the end of the day, limited only by its computational power, which capped at factorizing 143 (11x13). Thanks to iQuHack, I also succeeded at my other goal: I found an "in" to the world of quantum computing: developers, physicists, experimentalists, the community abuzz with excitement and optimism for our shared goals. It remains to be seen where I go from here, but a helpful article by Jenessa Duncombe paints quantum technologies into three broad strokes: computing, sensing and communication.

Quantum computing takes advantage of the ability of particles to be in a combination of states to perform simultaneous operations. This leads to an exponential speedup in certain types of problems. Knowing myself, this is a niche where I would excel at the physical layer, interacting with the qubits themselves and their control at the gates. The actual process of designing new algorithms, or deciding which gates implement a series of steps, is where I need to build up experience, which I hope to do over the next year (writing in Summer 2025). I will also need more experience at the programme-level: Using existing algorithms to solve today's problems.

Quantum sensing is the group of quantum technologies most concerned with metrology. This is where atomic clocks were birthed and will be the backbone of science for many decades to come. It is a discipline I know I can learn a lot from, such that I am able to get some hands on experience manipulating quantum particles. These skills should also be transferrable to manipulating qubits and improving quantum computers by improving state detection, which the industry already does tremendously well.

Quantum communication heralds a brand new age of security, both good and bad. The migration to quantum-safe algorithms is already underway, however, I am most interested in the channels along which qubits are ferried. This will most likely make use of photonic qubits to transmit information, but their states will decay over long distances, only making me all the more curious how quantum communication groups will overcome these challenges.

Broadly speaking, the biggest challenges facing quantum technologies today is the difficulty of preparing states, the difficulty of maintaining them, and creating enough reduncancy to sufficiently correct for errors. Depending on the qubit modality used, the relative prevalence of these and other issues varies wildly. However, the field still advances in spite of these challenges and improvements are constantly being made by different groups on different modes, which makes me very optimistic about the future of quantum technologies. It also gives me pause to think of not only what I can do for the field, but with the field.

There are several use cases for quantum technologies, from finance to chemistry. However, if I were to decide the direction of the entire industry, not for the most profitable cause, but based solely on personal preference, I think I would focus on Yottascale computing. This industry is uniquely positioned at a time where exascale cloud computing has generated revenue and floated entire economies. If quantum technologies could rival that software as a service, it would be able to handle even more loads which means that several companies who lack computational abilities of their own can simply purchase that capability. As a whole, this helps several other industries improve. Quantum technologies will be key to managing the enormous amount of data we generate each day.

In conclusion, I hope this essay gave a sufficient background on how I got involved in quantum technologies, why I want to be and above all demonstrates both my passion and ability to take part in writing its future. If convinced, please reach out to me and I will get back to you.
